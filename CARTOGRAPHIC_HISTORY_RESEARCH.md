## 1. Indigenous Cartography Erased by Colonial Mapping

### 1.1 Aboriginal Australian Songlines

**What happened:** Aboriginal Australians developed one of humanity's most sophisticated cartographic systems — the songline (or dreaming track). Songlines are paths across the continent traced by creator-beings during the Dreaming. They encode navigation, water sources, sacred sites, and ecological knowledge into oral song cycles. A knowledgeable person can navigate vast distances — including through deserts — by singing the song in the correct sequence, as the melodic contour and rhythm of the song describe the land's topography. Some songlines traverse 3,500 km crossing territories of multiple language groups, serving as "cultural passports." Anthropologist Robert Tonkinson (1978) documented that Mardu men, by learning songlines, "become familiar with literally thousands of sites even though they have never visited them; all become part of their cognitive map of the desert world."

**Why it's historically significant:** When British colonizers arrived, they found a continent that appeared unmapped to them because Aboriginal mapping was performative and oral — not inscribed on paper. Colonial surveyors erased these mapping traditions by imposing the cadastral grid, renaming landmarks, and declaring terra nullius. The knowledge encoded in songlines — water sources, seasonal food locations, sacred boundaries — was simply invisible to Western cartographic epistemology. This epistemological violence continues: mining companies still discover and bulldoze sacred sites that exist on no Western map but are vibrantly present in Aboriginal knowledge systems. The 2020 destruction of Juukan Gorge, a 46,000-year-old sacred site in Western Australia by Rio Tinto, is a direct contemporary example.

**PiggPin connection:** A P2P encrypted map where communities can annotate the physical world with their own knowledge — not as an alternative to the "official" map, but as a primary layer — directly addresses the epistemological violence of single-source cartography. Songlines demonstrate that the most important maps are sometimes sung, not drawn. PiggPin's "write-not-read" philosophy mirrors the performative nature of songlines: knowledge is held and transmitted within a trusted network, not broadcast for universal consumption. The palimpsest concept — where multiple layers of meaning coexist on the same territory — is literally what songlines are.

*Sources:* Tonkinson, R. (1978). *The Mardudjara Aborigines: Living The Dream In Australia's Desert.* Holt, Rinehart and Wilson. / Chatwin, B. (1987). *The Songlines.* The Viking Press. / Wikipedia, "Songline."

### 1.2 Marshall Islands Stick Charts

**What happened:** Marshallese navigators developed stick charts — physical maps made from coconut midribs lashed together, with shells tied to represent islands. These charts mapped not landmasses but ocean swell patterns: the *rilib* (strong northeast swell), *kaelib*, *bungdockerik*, and *bundockeing* swells, and how islands refracted and disrupted these swells. Each chart was functionally encrypted: "Individual charts varied so much in form and interpretation that the individual navigator who made the chart was the only person who could fully interpret and use it." Categories included *mattang* (abstract teaching charts), *meddo* (regional charts showing swell/island relationships), and *rebbelib* (comprehensive charts of entire island chains). Knowledge was passed father-to-son among a small class of navigators; squadrons of 15+ canoes sailed under a single pilot.

**Why it's historically significant:** Western navies could not comprehend these maps. Captain Winkler of the Imperial German Navy, stationed in the Marshall Islands in 1896, became "so intrigued by the stick charts that he made a major effort to determine navigational principles behind them and 'convinced' the navigators to share how the stick charts were used" (the scare quotes around 'convinced' appear in the source). The stick chart tradition ended after World War II due to electronic navigation, reduced canoe travel, and repeated US nuclear testing at Bikini Atoll which "led to widespread destruction by the US government of Marshall Island culture and stability." A mapping system that survived centuries of Pacific navigation was destroyed within a single generation by colonial technologies and military violence.

**PiggPin connection:** The stick charts demonstrate that effective maps can use entirely unfamiliar semiotic systems that outsiders cannot read — an extreme form of encryption-by-design. The navigator-centric model (only the maker can fully interpret) parallels how P2P encrypted data on piggPin could allow communities to maintain maps legible only to themselves. The destruction of the stick chart tradition by nuclear testing and colonial administration shows the existential threat that centralized power poses to local knowledge.

*Sources:* Ascher, M. (1995). "Models and maps from the Marshall Islands: A case in ethnomathematics." *Historia Mathematica*, 22, 347-370. / Lewis, D. (1994). *We, the Navigators: The Ancient Art of Landfinding in the Pacific.* University of Hawaii Press. / Wikipedia, "Marshall Islands stick chart."

### 1.3 Native American Territory Mapping & Māori Land Records

**What happened:** Pre-colonial North America was densely mapped. Indigenous nations maintained territorial boundaries, resource-access routes, and sacred site locations through oral tradition, wampum belts, petroglyphs, winter counts, and birchbark scrolls. The colonial project systematically replaced these with the Public Land Survey System (PLSS) — a rectilinear grid that ignored all existing spatial knowledge. Similarly, in Aotearoa/New Zealand, the Native Land Court (est. 1865) individualized collectively-held Māori land titles, requiring Māori to prove ownership through Western legal frameworks that did not recognize their mapping traditions. This resulted in the alienation of over 90% of Māori land by 1939.

**Why it's historically significant:** The cadastral grid was not neutral technology. It encoded a specific legal and economic framework — individual private property, taxable parcels, alienable land — that was fundamentally incompatible with indigenous systems of communal use, seasonal migration, and spiritual custodianship. As political geographer William Bunge wrote, maps do not simply describe territory; they produce it. The PLSS grid literally overwrote indigenous geographies with a framework designed for colonial settlement and resource extraction.

*Sources:* Goeman, M. (2013). *Mark My Words: Native Women Mapping Our Nations.* University of Minnesota Press. / Brody, H. (1981). *Maps and Dreams.* Douglas & McIntyre.

## 2. Counter-Cartography Movements

### 2.1 The Detroit Geographic Expedition and Institute (1968–1971)

**What happened:** In the aftermath of the 1967 Detroit rebellion, quantitative geographer William Bunge — a University of Washington PhD who had grown disillusioned with academic geography — formed the Detroit Geographic Expedition (DGE) in partnership with community organizer Gwendolyn Warren. Rather than "exploring" distant lands, the DGE explored its own neighborhood in inner-city Detroit. They produced maps of:
- Locations of pedestrian deaths from automobile strikes (which official maps omitted)
- Rat bites reported by residents (city health department refused to collect this data)
- Machine gun locations in the neighborhood (where heavy firepower was concentrated)
- School-to-child ratios showing systemic underinvestment in Black neighborhoods

The resulting 1969 publication *Atlas of Love and Hate* and 1971's *Fitzgerald: Geography of a Revolution* — a book about a single square mile in Detroit — were pathbreaking works of radical cartography. Students were trained in mapping and spatial analysis; the knowledge production was explicitly democratized. Bunge was subsequently blacklisted as a communist sympathizer and fled to Canada.

**Why it's historically significant:** The DGE demonstrated that communities could produce spatial knowledge that state agencies deliberately withheld or refused to collect. The maps were not merely informational — they were arguments for resource redistribution, safety interventions, and political accountability. The DGE pioneered what would later be called participatory GIS and counter-mapping, decades before the terms existed.

**PiggPin connection:** This is perhaps the closest historical precursor to piggPin's philosophy. The DGE community mapped what the state would not. Bunge's concept of "expeditions" into one's own neighborhood — rather than distant colonial exploration — inverts the power structure of cartography. PiggPin extends this logic with encryption: not only can communities map what power ignores, they can do so without the state knowing who mapped it.

### 2.2 ACT UP and AIDS Healthcare Access Mapping

**What happened:** The AIDS Coalition to Unleash Power (ACT UP), formed in 1987 in New York City, deployed mapping as a direct-action tool. Activists mapped:
- Locations of clinical trials and which populations were excluded from them
- Pharmacies that refused to fill prescriptions for people with AIDS
- Hospital emergency rooms that turned away AIDS patients
- The distribution of needle exchange programs versus locations of highest IV drug use
- Death rates overlaid with zip codes to demonstrate racial disparities in care access

These maps were used in direct actions — protests, sit-ins at pharmaceutical companies, and die-ins at government buildings. One of ACT UP's most powerful tactics was the "Body Count" map, which tracked AIDS deaths geographically and temporally to demonstrate government inaction.

**Why it's historically significant:** ACT UP's mapping was literally life-or-death. The maps proved, with spatial data, what the government denied: that AIDS was disproportionately killing gay men, Black people, and IV drug users in specific geographic clusters, and that healthcare access was being deliberately denied in those same clusters. This was counter-cartography as survival strategy.

*Sources:* Gould, P. (1993). *The Slow Plague: A Geography of the AIDS Pandemic.* Blackwell. / Crimp, D. (1990). *AIDS Demographics.* Bay Press. / ACT UP Oral History Project, actuporalhistory.org.

## 3. Maps as Tools of Dispossession

### 3.1 The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494)

**What happened:** On 7 June 1494, Pope Alexander VI (a Spaniard by birth) mediated the Treaty of Tordesillas between Portugal and Castile, drawing a meridian 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands. Everything newly "discovered" to the west would belong to Castile; east to Portugal. The treaty divided a hemisphere neither power had seen — and its signatories never consulted a single indigenous person living on the lands being divided. The line was drawn through what neither party could even accurately measure; as one scholar assessed, "both sides must have known that so vague a boundary could not be accurately fixed, and each thought that the other was deceived." Yet it held force for centuries. The Treaty of Zaragoza (1529) extended the logic to the Pacific, establishing an anti-meridian that divided the Moluccas. King Francis I of France captured the absurdity: "The sun shines for me as it does for others. I would very much like to see the clause of Adam's will by which I should be denied my share of the world."

**Why it's historically significant:** This is the ur-example of map-as-dispossession. Two European powers, acting through papal authority, drew a line on a map and thereby claimed sovereignty over peoples, lands, and resources they had never encountered. The line was physically unenforceable (the Spanish did not resist Portuguese expansion of Brazil across the meridian) but legally binding — demonstrating that cartographic fiction, when backed by institutional power, becomes territorial reality. This precedent established the foundational logic of European colonialism: the map precedes and produces the territory.

**PiggPin connection:** A P2P map that encrypts local knowledge ensures no distant authority can draw lines through territories whose inhabitants have no representation. The Treaty of Tordesillas is the counter-example against which piggPin's philosophy stands: if maps can be owned by those who live on the land rather than those who claim it from afar, the very structure of colonial spatial power is inverted.

*Sources:* Davenport, F. G. (1917). *European Treaties Bearing on the History of the United States and Its Dependencies.* Carnegie Institution. / Parry, J.H. (1973). *The Age of Reconnaissance.* University of California Press. / Wikipedia, "Treaty of Tordesillas."

### 3.2 The Berlin Conference (1884–1885)

**What happened:** From 15 November 1884 to 26 February 1885, fourteen European powers and the United States met at Otto von Bismarck's invitation in Berlin. Their stated purpose was to regulate European colonization and trade in Africa. The General Act of Berlin formalized the ongoing "Scramble for Africa." The conference established the "Principle of Effective Occupation" — a European power could claim African territory if it had treaties with local leaders (often coercive or fraudulent), flew its flag, and established an administration. Crucially, no African representatives attended. The borders drawn at the conference and in subsequent bilateral agreements were straight lines, ignoring ethnic boundaries, linguistic groups, trade routes, seasonal migration patterns, and political territories that had existed for centuries. European powers divided Africa between 1870 and 1914 from roughly 10% to 90% control.

**Why it's historically significant:** The Berlin Conference's straight-line borders are the most visible cartographic violence in history. The borders produced enduring conflicts because they divided ethnic groups across states and forced rival groups into the same state. The borders drawn in Berlin still govern African nation-states today. What appeared on the European conference room map as tidy administrative divisions materialized as centuries of violence, displacement, and political instability for millions of people.

**PiggPin connection:** The Berlin Conference demonstrates that maps do not passively describe territory — they actively produce it. A map drawn in Berlin became the legal reality on the ground in Africa. PiggPin's community-ownership model directly counters this logic: instead of a room of Europeans deciding what territory "is" from thousands of miles away, the people who live on the land produce the map. The palimpsest concept is especially powerful here — piggPin could show the pre-colonial territories, the colonial borders, and the contemporary nation-state boundaries as layered, contested inscriptions.

*Sources:* Crowe, S.E. (1942). *The Berlin West African Conference, 1884–1885.* Longmans, Green. / Pakenham, T. (1991). *The Scramble for Africa.* Random House. / Wikipedia, "Berlin Conference."

### 3.3 The Dawes Act (1887) — Cadastral Surveys as Dispossession

**What happened:** The Dawes Act (General Allotment Act of 1887), signed by President Grover Cleveland, authorized the US President to subdivide Native American tribal communal lands into individual allotments for heads of families and individuals. Its stated purpose was to "civilize" Native Americans by forcing them into individual private property, breaking up tribal governance, and opening "surplus" land to white settlement. Between 1887 and 1934, Native Americans lost approximately 90 million acres of treaty land — roughly two-thirds of the land base they held in 1887, shrinking from 138 million acres to 48 million acres. Senator Henry Dawes himself later remarked: "I never knew a White man to get his foot on an Indian's land who ever took it off."

The Dawes Act also functioned as an identity-annihilation tool. It created blood-quantum classifications ("full-blood" vs. "mixed-blood"), with "mixed-blood" individuals receiving better land but forced into US citizenship and tribal status relinquishment, while "full-blood" individuals received smaller, government-controlled parcels. Those who didn't fit either category were "detribalized" — removed from official Indigenous identity entirely. The mapping instrument — cadastral survey — literally wrote people off the map of recognized existence.

**Why it's historically significant:** This is cartographic dispossession executed through the surveyor's chain and the politician's ledger. Individual allotment destroyed communal land tenure not accidentally but by design. The cadastral grid inscribed a property regime fundamentally incompatible with Indigenous cultural systems, and the 25-year trust period ensured the land would eventually be alienated. Recent research has found the Dawes Act caused increases in child mortality and decreases in life expectancy of about 20% in native communities. The subsequent problem of land fractionation — where some parcels now have ownership interests of less than 0.0000001% — is a direct legacy of this cartographic violence still unresolved today.

*Sources:* Carlson, L.A. (1981). *Indians, Bureaucrats, and Land.* Greenwood Press. / Wikipedia, "Dawes Act." / Hodel v. Irving, 481 U.S. 704 (1987) — describing a 40-acre tract with 439 owners and a common denominator of 3,394,923,840,000.

## 4. Community Mapping That Preserved Erased History

### 4.1 The 1921 Tulsa Race Massacre — Survivor Mapping

**What happened:** On May 31–June 1, 1921, a white mob — some appointed as deputies by city officials — attacked the Greenwood District in Tulsa, Oklahoma, known as "Black Wall Street." They burned and destroyed more than 35 square blocks of one of the wealthiest Black communities in the United States. Between 39 and 300 people were killed; over 800 were hospitalized; approximately 10,000 were left homeless; 6,000 Black residents were interned in detention camps. The massacre was subsequently erased from public records and omitted from local, state, and national histories for decades. It was not until 1997 that the Oklahoma state legislature authorized an official commission to study the event.

Survivor testimony — including oral histories, personal maps of destroyed businesses, and hand-drawn neighborhood reconstructions — preserved knowledge that official institutions suppressed. Mary E. Jones Parrish, a survivor, documented the massacre in her 1922 book *Events of the Tulsa Disaster.* The 2001 Commission report relied heavily on these community-produced spatial records to reconstruct what happened. Additionally, the ongoing search for mass graves (which began yielding physical evidence only in the 2020s) has relied on survivor-drawn maps of where bodies were buried — locations that existed in Black community knowledge but nowhere in official records.

**Why it's historically significant:** This is a textbook case of official cartographic erasure followed by community counter-mapping as historical recovery. The city government, newspapers, and historical societies suppressed spatial knowledge about the massacre. Survivors preserved it. The search for mass graves, the reparations claims, and the eventual inclusion of the massacre in Oklahoma school curricula (mandated in 2002) all depend on the community mapping that was done when the state was actively destroying evidence.

*Sources:* Oklahoma Commission to Study the Tulsa Race Riot of 1921 (2001). Final Report. / Ellsworth, S. (1992). *Death in a Promised Land.* Louisiana State University Press. / Wikipedia, "Tulsa race massacre."

### 4.2 The 1985 MOVE Bombing — Philadelphia

**What happened:** On May 13, 1985, the Philadelphia Police Department dropped two bombs from a helicopter onto the MOVE organization's communal house at 6221 Osage Avenue. The resulting fire killed six MOVE members and five of their children, and destroyed 65 neighboring houses — an entire city block. The Philadelphia Fire Department admitted under oath that they let the fire burn. The city rebuilt the site into new housing, and the physical evidence was demolished. No police or city officials were criminally convicted. The site became a spatial lacuna in Philadelphia's urban fabric — a destroyed neighborhood whose destruction was legally suppressed.

For decades after, local residents maintained the spatial memory of the bombing — who lived where, where the bombs fell, where bodies were found, which houses burned first. Community-produced maps and oral testimony preserved the geography of the event that the city attempted to erase through physical demolition and legal settlement. In 1996, MOVE survivors won a $1.5 million civil settlement; displaced residents won $12.83 million in 2005.

**Why it's historically significant:** The MOVE bombing demonstrates that state violence against communities is not just physical but cartographic. The city bombed a neighborhood, let it burn, bulldozed the remains, rebuilt with different street numbers, and attempted to erase the event from spatial records. Community mapping — both the memory of the destroyed block and the documentation of what happened — preserved what official records suppressed.

*Sources:* Wagner-Pacifici, R. (1994). *Discourse and Destruction: The City of Philadelphia versus MOVE.* University of Chicago Press. / Wikipedia, "MOVE (Philadelphia organization)."

### 4.3 Favela Mapping in Rio de Janeiro

**What happened:** Rio de Janeiro's favelas — informal settlements housing approximately 25% of the city's population — were for decades literally absent from official maps. The municipal government did not recognize favelas as formal neighborhoods, meaning they appeared as blank spaces on official city maps. Without official recognition, residents could not receive mail, access city services, prove residency, or claim property rights. In response, residents organized their own mapping initiatives. The most prominent, *Wikimapa* (initiated around 2009), used crowdsourced mapping to document favela streets, addresses, businesses, and community institutions — creating a digital map where official cartography showed nothing.

Google Maps later entered this space, selectively mapping some favelas — but the mapping was incomplete and, in some cases, harmful; cartel-enforced barriers and police occupation zones were mapped interchangeably, creating a surveillance surface that served neither residents' privacy nor safety needs. In 2009, Rio's state government launched the UPP (Pacifying Police Unit) program, which used mapping as a tool of military occupation — identifying entry points, choke points, and "gang-controlled" zones.

**Why it's historically significant:** Favelas demonstrate that being absent from the map is a form of violence (no services, no legal recognition, no mail) — but being mapped by the state is also a form of violence (surveillance for military occupation). The residents' own mapping efforts navigated between these two violences: creating spatial legibility in a way that served the community rather than the police.

**PiggPin connection:** This is a perfect illustration of piggPin's use case. Residents want spatial data to exist (for services, addresses, community organizing) but do not want that data owned or surveilled by the state. P2P encrypted mapping solves this paradox: the data exists within the trusted community network and is cryptographically protected from external surveillance. Favela residents could map their own neighborhood on piggPin without the state knowing who has what information.

*Sources:* Perlman, J. (2010). *Favela: Four Decades of Living on the Edge in Rio de Janeiro.* Oxford University Press. / Wikipedia, "Favela."

## 5. Environmental Justice Mapping

### 5.1 Cancer Alley, Louisiana

**What happened:** "Cancer Alley" is an 85-mile stretch along the Mississippi River between Baton Rouge and New Orleans containing over 200 petrochemical plants and refineries — the largest concentration of such facilities in the Western Hemisphere. The area accounts for approximately 25% of US petrochemical production. In the 1980s, residents of Chalmette, Louisiana — a predominantly Black community — began calling their area "Cancer Alley" after observing cancer rates far exceeding national averages. The EPA found the region's air exposes residents to cancer risks 47 times greater than acceptable federal thresholds. In one notorious area near the Denka/DuPont neoprene plant in St. John the Baptist Parish, the cancer risk from air pollution is over 700 times the national average. That single plant emits 99% of the nation's chloroprene pollution.

Since the 1990s, community organizations — St. James Citizens for the Environment, the Louisiana Bucket Brigade, Rise St. James (led by Goldman Prize winner Sharon Lavigne) — have conducted their own air quality monitoring, produced community-generated maps of toxic release sites, and correlated them with health outcome data. These community maps have been critical in legal challenges that have stopped multiple proposed petrochemical expansions, including Shintech's PVC plants (1998), Formosa Plastics' rayon plant (1992), and Formosa's Sunshine Project (ongoing). In January 2021, President Biden signed an executive order specifically citing Cancer Alley as an environmental justice priority area. In March 2021, the UN Human Rights Committee addressed Cancer Alley, condemning what it defined as environmental racism.

**Why it's historically significant:** Cancer Alley is arguably the most prominent environmental justice site in the United States, and community-produced spatial data has been the primary tool of resistance. The state's Louisiana Tumor Registry, which industry cites to claim there is no elevated cancer risk, uses geographic units too large to detect neighborhood-level clusters. Only community-scale mapping has been able to demonstrate the relationship between specific industrial sites and specific health outcomes.

**PiggPin connection:** Environmental monitoring data is intrinsically spatial — and in communities like Cancer Alley, it is existential. A P2P encrypted map where residents can log air quality readings, health incidents, and industrial violations without government or corporate intermediary is a direct extension of the bucket brigade model into the digital realm. Encryption protects monitors and whistleblowers from retaliation by the industry that dominates local employment.

*Sources:* Blodgett, A.D. (2006). "An Analysis of Pollution and Community Advocacy in 'Cancer Alley.'" *Local Environment*, 11(6), 647-661. / Human Rights Watch (2024). "We're Dying Here: The Fight for Life in a Louisiana Fossil Fuel Sacrifice Zone." / Wikipedia, "Cancer Alley."

### 5.2 Flint Water Crisis — Community Science Mapping

**What happened:** In April 2014, a state-appointed emergency manager switched Flint, Michigan's water supply from Lake Huron (via Detroit) to the Flint River. The water was not treated with corrosion inhibitors, causing lead from aging pipes to leach into the drinking water. Between 6,000 and 14,000 children were exposed to elevated lead levels. State and federal officials repeatedly denied the problem. MDEQ spokesman Brad Wurfel told Michigan Radio in July 2015: "Anyone who is concerned about lead in the drinking water in Flint can relax."

The truth was uncovered not by government agencies but by community-led science:
- Flint resident **LeeAnne Walters** collected water samples from her home showing lead levels seven times the EPA action level (detected by EPA manager Miguel Del Toral, who wrote an internal memo that was suppressed)
- **Virginia Tech** professor Marc Edwards and his team conducted a citizen-science water study, sampling hundreds of Flint homes and finding 40% had elevated lead levels — data the state had refused to collect
- Pediatrician **Mona Hanna-Attisha** at Hurley Medical Center mapped blood lead levels in children before and after the water switch, demonstrating a statistically significant increase — a finding state officials initially attacked as "irresponsible"
- The combined community-data effort forced state and national acknowledgment; President Obama declared a federal emergency in January 2016

The spatial dimension was critical: the blood-lead-level maps overlaid with water infrastructure data proved that the lead exposure was not random but directly tied to the water source change. This was citizen counter-cartography that exposed a public health crime.

**Sources:** Hanna-Attisha, M. (2018). *What the Eyes Don't See.* One World. / Wikipedia, "Flint water crisis."

### 5.3 Fukushima Daiichi — Safecast Citizen Radiation Mapping

**What happened:** After the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster on 11 March 2011, the Japanese government and TEPCO released radiation data that was widely considered incomplete, delayed, and lacking granularity. In response, three founders — Sean Bonner, Joi Ito, and Pieter Franken — launched **Safecast** the very next day, on 12 March 2011. Safecast is an international volunteer-driven nonprofit that built and deployed the *bGeigie Nano* — an open-hardware, GPS-enabled mobile radiation sensor used primarily for car-borne mapping across Japan.

By January 2020, Safecast had accumulated more than **120 million observations**, making it the largest open dataset of background radiation measurements ever collected. Independent validation confirmed Safecast data was highly correlated with US Department of Energy aerial survey data — meaning citizen science matched or exceeded government data quality. In 2022, following the Russian invasion of Ukraine, Safecast launched the *bGeigies for Ukraine* initiative, deploying sensors in Chernobyl exclusion-zone areas after Russian troop withdrawal, gathering over 300,000 radiation data points.

**Why it's historically significant:** Safecast is the most successful citizen-science environmental mapping project in history. It demonstrated that when governments withhold or manipulate spatial environmental data — whether from incompetence (Japan in 2011), corporate capture (TEPCO), or active conflict (Ukraine in 2022) — a decentralized network of volunteers with open-source hardware can produce higher-quality data faster than centralized agencies. Safecast publishes all data under CC0 (public domain) and maintains a public API and interactive map.

**PiggPin connection:** Safecast is piggPin's closest living relative in the environmental domain. The Safecast model — open hardware, volunteer data collection, open data publication — maps almost directly onto piggPin's philosophy, with one addition: piggPin adds **encryption** and **P2P architecture**, which Safecast does not have. In contexts where radiation data is politically sensitive (Fukushima displacement zones, Chernobyl after Russian occupation, future nuclear incidents), encrypted P2P data collection would protect volunteers and residents from retaliation.

"Safecast: successful citizen-science for radiation measurement and communication after Fukushima." *Journal of Radiological Protection*, 36(2), S82-S101. / Coletti, M. et al. (2017). ## 6. Disaster Response Mutual Aid Mapping

### 6.1 Hurricane Katrina — The Cajun Navy and Informal Maps (2005)

**What happened:** Hurricane Katrina made landfall on 29 August 2005 as a Category 3 hurricane. Eighty percent of New Orleans flooded due to 53 levee breaches. FEMA's official response was catastrophic — director Michael Brown resigned amid criticism, and 1,392 people died (revised figure from 2023). In the absence of functional official response, the "Cajun Navy" — an informal flotilla of private citizens with boats — conducted water rescues using improvised navigation. They shared information about flooded streets, stranded people, and accessible routes through decentralized, ad-hoc communication channels: CB radios, cell phones, word of mouth. There was no central map. The official maps (when available) were outdated or inaccurate due to rapidly changing flood conditions. The informal mapping network, maintained through mutual aid, saved thousands of lives.

**Why it's historically significant:** Katrina demonstrated that in a major disaster, centralized mapping infrastructure fails catastrophically — not just because of technical failure but because of bureaucratic dysfunction. The Cajun Navy's decentralized mapping — each boat crew maintaining partial knowledge of their sector, shared through a mesh network of communication — was not an inferior substitute for a centralized map but actually a more resilient, more rapidly updating system. It was P2P mapping in practice, decades before the technology existed.

*Sources:* Brinkley, D. (2006). *The Great Deluge: Hurricane Katrina, New Orleans, and the Mississippi Gulf Coast.* HarperCollins. / Wikipedia, "Hurricane Katrina."

### 6.2 2010 Haiti Earthquake — OpenStreetMap Volunteers Outperform Agencies

**What happened:** On 12 January 2010, a magnitude 7.0 earthquake struck Haiti, killing an estimated 100,000–160,000 people and affecting three million. Within hours, OpenStreetMap (OSM) volunteers around the world began tracing satellite imagery — donated by GeoEye, DigitalGlobe, and others — to produce the first comprehensive digital map of Port-au-Prince and surrounding areas. Before the earthquake, much of Haiti was unmapped on any digital platform. Within 48 hours, OSM volunteers had produced a detailed street-level map of the affected area, including collapsed bridges, displaced-persons camps, functioning hospitals, and blocked roads. The UN, USAID, the World Bank, and search-and-rescue teams all relied on OSM data because no official agency had comparable geospatial intelligence. This was the first major deployment of the Humanitarian OpenStreetMap Team (HOT).

**Why it's historically significant:** The Haiti earthquake response was the watershed moment for crowdsourced disaster mapping. It proved that a decentralized network of volunteers, given satellite imagery and a collaborative platform, could produce higher-quality, more rapidly updated maps than any government or international agency. The model has since been replicated for every major humanitarian disaster. It fundamentally shifted the epistemology of disaster response mapping from "the state produces the map and distributes it down" to "the network produces the map and shares it laterally."

**PiggPin connection:** The OSM Haiti response is the positive-proof case for P2P mapping infrastructure during disasters. The limitation of the OSM model is that it requires a centralized platform (openstreetmap.org) and publicly visible data. PiggPin's P2P encrypted architecture could extend this model into contexts where the mapping itself is politically sensitive — refugee routes, dissident safe houses, or disaster response in authoritarian states where the government suppresses information about affected areas.

## 7. The Danger of Single-Source Maps

### 7.1 Google Maps Erasing Palestinian Villages

**What happened:** Since roughly 2016, Google Maps has been documented as systematically underrepresenting Palestinian geography in the occupied West Bank. Users have reported that Palestinian village names, roads, and place markers are absent from Google Maps while Israeli settlements — illegal under international law — are clearly labeled and navigable. The maps do not show the Green Line (1949 armistice line), making it impossible for users to distinguish between Israel proper and the occupied territories. Google has alternately claimed this is due to "data sources" and acknowledged removing labels after user abuse — but the effect is the erasure of Palestinian spatial existence from the world's most-used mapping platform. In 2020, a petition by Palestinians and allied groups catalogued specific villages, roads, and businesses that had been removed. Google's inconsistent policies around "disputed territories" create a map that naturalizes one side's territorial claims while rendering the other side invisible.

**Why it's historically significant:** When the world's default map — used by over 1 billion monthly active users — makes a population spatially illegible, the political consequences are profound. Tourists navigate to Israeli settlements unaware they are in occupied territory. Humanitarian organizations cannot locate Palestinian villages using the default map. The cumulative effect is the normalization of occupation through cartographic infrastructure. This is not about a map being "wrong" — it is about a single-source map exercising sovereignty-by-platform.

**PiggPin connection:** This is the most immediate contemporary illustration of why single-source maps are dangerous — not despite their accuracy in other contexts but because of their monopoly position. If Google Maps were one layer among many, its political choices would be contestable. Because it is the default, its choices are infrastructural. piggPin's multi-source, community-owned architecture means no single entity can decide which places "exist" — every community maintains its own markers, and the map becomes a contested surface rather than a pronouncement.

*Sources:* B'Tselem (Israeli human rights organization). Reports on West Bank geography. / Amnesty International (2020). Reports on the erasure of Palestinian villages from digital platforms. / Additional reporting in The Guardian, Al Jazeera, and The Intercept (2016-2020).

### 7.2 Crimea Place-Name Erasure After 2014 Annexation

**What happened:** Following Russia's 2014 annexation of Crimea, Russian authorities systematically renamed Ukrainian place names. Simferopol's streets were renamed to remove Ukrainian and Crimean Tatar names; Sevastopol's city administration published official maps with exclusively Russian toponymy. Simultaneously, digital mapping platforms faced competing demands: Yandex (Russian) showed Russian names; Google Maps and Apple Maps varied by region — showing different names depending on which country the user accessed from. From Ukraine, the maps showed Ukrainian names; from Russia, Russian names; from third countries, inconsistent and fluctuating naming. In some disputed territory cases, Apple Maps literally showed different borders depending on the user's IP address, effectively creating geopolitically customized cartographic realities.

**Why it's historically significant:** The Crimea example demonstrates that digital maps are now active instruments of territorial contestation. The name is the claim. When a platform switches what it calls a city depending on who's asking, it is not being neutral — it is both recognizing and reinforcing the territorial claims embedded in each name. The problem is not that the map takes sides; the problem is the pretense that there is a single authoritative map when in fact there are multiple irreconcilable geographic truths. The palimpsest — showing all the names, layered — would be more honest than any single-answer map.

*Sources:* UN General Assembly Resolution 68/262 (2014) affirming Ukraine's territorial integrity. / Coverage in Reuters, BBC, and The Verge (2014-2022) on digital mapping and Crimea.

### 7.3 The Apple Maps Border Problem

**What happened:** On multiple occasions, Apple Maps has displayed incorrect borders that caused diplomatic incidents. In one incident, Apple Maps showed the Crimean peninsula as belonging to Russia when accessed from within Russia, but as part of Ukraine when accessed elsewhere — geo-IP conditional cartography that made the map's political position transparently inconsistent. In disputed regions including Kashmir, the South China Sea, and Western Sahara, Apple Maps has been pressured by governments to display specific border configurations, leading to a patchwork of region-specific cartographic fictions.

**Why it's historically significant:** When a platform serves different maps to different users based on IP geolocation, it reveals the fundamental impossibility of a single authoritative map in a world of contested territories. The "right answer" depends on who is asking. This is not a technical bug — it is a political feature of centralized mapping. The platform must choose whose geography to privilege, and that choice is always political. A P2P map that allows multiple annotations on the same territory eliminates the pretense that there needs to be a single answer — or a single corporation deciding it.

## SYNTHESIS: What These Histories Mean for piggPin

The historical record reveals a consistent pattern across centuries and continents:

1. **Maps are never neutral.** Every map encodes a worldview, a property regime, a set of exclusions. The colonial grid, the cadastral survey, the Berlin Conference's straight lines — these were not attempts at objective description but instruments of power that produced the territory they claimed to merely record.

2. **Centralized map control enables dispossession.** From the Treaty of Tordesillas to the Dawes Act to Google's erasure of Palestinian villages, the concentration of mapping authority in a single institution has consistently resulted in the erasure of marginalized communities. The map precedes the territory; whoever controls the map controls what counts as real.

3. **Communities counter-map to survive.** The Detroit Geographic Expedition, ACT UP, the Louisiana Bucket Brigade, Safecast, favela residents, Tulsa massacre survivors — in every case, communities produced their own spatial data because official institutions refused to, or because official maps actively erased them. Counter-mapping is not academic critique; it is survival strategy.

4. **Disaster response proves P2P mapping works.** The Cajun Navy, OpenStreetMap Haiti, and Safecast all demonstrated that decentralized, volunteer-driven mapping networks can outperform centralized agencies in speed, granularity, and reliability. The infrastructure works.

5. **Single-source maps are single points of failure and control.** Google Maps, Apple Maps, and Yandex Maps each enforce a specific geopolitical reality based on corporate policy and state pressure. The user has no way to see competing claims, no way to annotate their own knowledge, and no way to verify what has been suppressed. A palimpsest — a multi-layered map showing all inscriptions — is not a design choice but an epistemological necessity.

**piggPin's architecture answers each of these historical lessons:**

- **P2P:** No central server, no single entity that can be pressured, bought, or bombed into changing the map
- **Encrypted:** Communities can maintain spatial data without surveillance — critical for favela residents, environmental whistleblowers, and political dissidents
- **Community-owned:** The people who live on the land produce the map; external authorities cannot draw lines through uninhabited regions
- **Write-not-read:** The model prioritizes local knowledge inscription over global consumption — like songlines, knowledge is held within trusted networks
- **Palimpsest:** Multiple, potentially conflicting geographic inscriptions coexist — not resolved into a single "correct" answer, but presented as layers that reveal the contestation over territory itself
