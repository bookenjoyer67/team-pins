## 1. Pre-Colonial Mapping Traditions Erased or Misunderstood by Colonial Powers {#1}

### 1.1 Australian Aboriginal Songlines (Dreaming Tracks)

**Dates:** 50,000+ years of continuous practice

**Places:** Australia-wide. Key examples:
- A **3,500 km songline** connecting the Central Desert Region to Byron Bay on the east coast (approx. coordinates: from **-25.0, 133.0** to **-28.6, 153.6**)
- Uluru (**-25.3444, 131.0369**) — a major songline intersection point
- Wollemi National Park, NSW (**-33.0, 150.5**) — songlines linked to Aboriginal rock art sites

**What they are:** Songlines are paths across land and sky marked by creator-beings during the Dreaming. Each songline is a navigable route encoded in song, story, dance, and art. The songs describe landmarks, waterholes, and natural phenomena in sequence. By singing in the correct order, Aboriginal people could navigate across hundreds of kilometers of desert.

**Specific example of erasure:** When British colonists arrived in 1788, they found no "maps" in the European sense — no paper, no grid, no lines of latitude. The songlines were invisible to them. The legal doctrine of *terra nullius* (nobody's land) declared Australia "desert and uninhabited" despite 250+ distinct language groups, complex economies, and an intricate continent-spanning network of navigational knowledge encoded in song cycles. In the Mabo case (1992), Justice Dawson acknowledged: "From the inception of the colony, the Crown treated all land in the colony as unoccupied and afforded no recognition to any form of native interest in the land."

**Why it matters for piggPin:** Songlines are a P2P mapping system *avant la lettre* — knowledge shared between language groups, each knowing their section of the song. The "palimpsest" concept is literal here: songlines are layered knowledge (geographic, spiritual, ecological, legal) stored in a non-written, encrypted-by-culture format that only initiates could decode.

### 1.2 Marshall Islands Stick Charts

**Dates:** Pre-colonial through WWII (practice largely ended after US nuclear testing at Bikini Atoll, 1946–1958)

**Places:** Marshall Islands, Micronesia. Approx. coordinates: **7.1, 171.4** (Majuro)

**What they are:** Stick charts (*mattang*, *meddo*, and *rebbelib*) made from coconut frond midribs and cowrie shells, representing ocean swell patterns and how islands disrupt them. Different from Western maps: they encoded *wave refraction patterns*, not spatial distances. Navigators memorized the charts before voyages, then navigated by *feeling* how swells pitched the canoe.

**Three types:**
- **Mattang:** Abstract instructional charts teaching swell principles
- **Meddo:** Charts of specific island chains with swell patterns
- **Rebbelib:** Comprehensive charts covering entire island chains

**Specific example of erasure:** The system was jealously guarded within navigator families (father to son). Only a few rulers knew the method. When German naval officer Captain Winkler of SMS *Bussard* comprehensively documented the system in the 1890s, it had already begun to decline. The US nuclear testing program (67 tests at Bikini and Enewetak, 1946–1958) forcibly relocated Marshallese communities, disrupting the oral transmission of navigational knowledge. WWII accelerated the decline through the introduction of electronic navigation. The knowledge was nearly lost — only a handful of people today understand the full system.

**Why it matters for piggPin:** Stick charts prove that maps don't need to represent physical space in Cartesian terms. They encoded *processes* (wave dynamics), not objects. This aligns with piggPin's philosophy of community-owned, non-standardized mapping. The stick chart is a physical palimpsest — each chart could only be fully read by its maker.

### 1.3 Inca Quipus as Spatial Records

**Dates:** c. 2600 BCE (proto-quipus at Caral) through c. 1532 CE (Spanish conquest). Contemporary ceremonial use continues in villages like Tupicocha, San Cristóbal de Rapaz, and Jucul, Peru.

**Places:** Central Andes. Key sites:
- Cusco (**-13.5320, -71.9675**) — Inca capital
- Puruchuco, near Lima (**-12.0464, -76.9350**) — archaeological site with quipu evidence
- Tupicocha, Peru (**-12.0333, -76.5167**) — where quipus remain in active ceremonial use (documented by Frank Salomon, 1994)
- San Cristóbal de Rapaz, Peru (**-10.8833, -76.6333**) — village quipu kept in *Kaha Wayi* ceremonial building

**What they are:** Knotted cord recording devices using a decimal positional system. Quipus recorded census data, tax obligations, military organization, calendrical information, and potentially narrative histories and "annales" (historical chronicles). The *quipucamayocs* (knot specialists) could perform arithmetic and read information by touch. Quipus used color coding, knot types, cord attachment orientation, and fiber types as information carriers.

**Recorded spatial functions:** Quipus tracked *mita* (territorial labor tax obligations), managed resource distribution across provinces, recorded census by region, and may have been used to record toponyms (place names). A 2005 Science paper identified a potential quipu element functioning as a place-name for Puruchuco.

**Specific example of erasure:** The Third Council of Lima (1583) banned and ordered the burning of quipus as "idolatrous objects" that recorded offerings to non-Christian gods. Of an estimated tens of thousands, only ~1,400 pre-Columbian quipus survive today in museum collections. The Spanish systematically destroyed Andean information infrastructure the same way they burned Maya codices. The largest known collection (298 quipus) is held at the Ethnological Museum of Berlin — removed from Peru.

**Why it matters for piggPin:** Quipus are an encrypted information storage system using knotted cord — a physical, non-digital, decentralized information architecture. Only the maker/keeper could fully decode them. This is a 500-year-old precedent for encrypted, community-controlled data. The quipu's multiple simultaneous encoding dimensions (color, knot type, spatial position, fiber) mirror the layered data model of a palimpsest mapping system.

- Marcia Ascher & Robert Ascher, *Code of the Quipu* (1981)
- Frank Salomon, *The Cord Keepers* (2004), Duke University Press
- Sabine Hyland on epistolary quipus from San Juan de Collata (colonial-era phonetic decipherment claim)

### 1.4 Native American Winter Counts, Wampum Belts, and Petroglyphs

**Winter Counts (Lakota: *waníyetu wówapi*)**

**Dates:** Continuous records spanning centuries. Known winter counts include:
- Lone Dog's winter count (Yanktonai Sioux), covering 1800–1871
- Battiste Good's winter count (Brulé Lakota), covering 1700s–1880s
- The Flame's winter count, The Swan's winter count, Major Bush's winter count
- Kiowa winter counts by Tohausen, Silver Horn (1860–1940), and Anko (1864–1892)

**Places:** Northern Great Plains:
- Standing Rock Sioux Reservation (**45.75, -101.2**) — Dakota Access Pipeline protest site (2016)
- Pine Ridge Reservation (**43.0, -102.5**)
- Kiowa lands, Oklahoma (**35.0, -98.5**)
- Smithsonian Institution, Washington DC — houses major winter count collections

**What they are:** Pictorial calendars traditionally painted on bison hides, with one pictograph per year depicting the year's most memorable event. They served as mnemonic devices for oral history traditions. The keeper (always male, passing to a chosen successor) would consult tribal elders to reach consensus on naming each year.

**Territorial significance:** Winter counts recorded epidemics (smallpox, measles — evidence of colonial contact impacts), encounters with settlers, treaty signings, inter-band relations, resource conditions, and movement patterns. They are de facto territorial records that document which bands were where and when.

**Key verifying event:** Smithsonian scholar Garrick Mallery used the "Year the Stars Fell" entry (the Leonid meteor storm of November 1833) to correlate Lakota winter counts with the Western calendar, proving their chronological accuracy.

**Wampum belts (Haudenosaunee/Iroquois):** Made from quahog shell beads, wampum belts recorded treaties, land agreements, and political histories. The Two Row Wampum (1613) recorded the agreement between the Haudenosaunee and Dutch — two vessels traveling the river together but not interfering with each other, representing parallel sovereignty.

**Petroglyphs as territorial markers:** Sydney Rock Engravings (NSW, Australia) and petroglyphs throughout North America (e.g., Petroglyph National Monument, New Mexico, **35.1389, -106.7133**) served as territorial boundary markers and records of resource locations, travel routes, and sacred sites.

**Erasure:** Winter counts on bison hides were lost as bison were systematically exterminated in the 19th century (from ~30-60 million to fewer than 1,000 by 1890). Colonial collectors commissioned copies on muslin and paper, removing them from their communities. The transition from hide to Western materials represents a colonial intervention in the record-keeping medium itself.

**Sources:**
- Wikipedia: "Winter count" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_count
- Greene & Thornton, *The Year the Stars Fell: Lakota Winter Counts at the Smithsonian* (2007)
- Smithsonian "Lakota Winter Counts: An Online Exhibit" — https://wintercounts.si.edu
- Hansen, *Memory and Vision: Arts, Cultures, and Lives of Plains Indian People* (2007)

### 1.5 Polynesian Navigation Charts and Oral Mapping

**Dates:** c. 1500 BCE through present (see Hōkūleʻa voyaging canoe revivals, 1976–present)

**Places:** Pacific-wide, specifically:
- Hawaii (**20.0, -156.0**) — traditional *ahupuaʻa* land division system
- Tahiti (**-17.65, -149.43**)
- Rarotonga, Cook Islands (**-21.23, -159.78**)
- Aotearoa/New Zealand (**-40.9, 174.9**)

**What they are:** Polynesian navigation combined star compasses, ocean swell reading, bird migration patterns, cloud formations, and oral recitation of voyage routes. The navigator Mau Piailug (from Satawal, Micronesia) taught the art of traditional navigation to the Polynesian Voyaging Society, leading to the revival of long-distance voyaging without instruments.

**Key people:** Mau Piailug (1932–2010), Nainoa Thompson (navigator of Hōkūleʻa)

**Erasure:** European colonizers dismissed oral navigation traditions as primitive. Missionaries suppressed indigenous knowledge systems. The colonization of Hawaii (1898) and Aotearoa/New Zealand (1840 Treaty of Waitangi) imposed Western land tenure systems that ignored traditional Hawaiian *ahupuaʻa* (watershed-based land divisions from mountain to sea) and Māori tribal territories.

### 1.6 West African Mapping Traditions and Mansa Musa

**Dates:** 14th century (Mali Empire at its height under Mansa Musa, r. 1312–1337)

**Places:** Timbuktu, Mali (**16.7666, -3.0026**), Djenne (**13.9054, -4.5550**), Gao (**16.2667, -0.0500**)

**The Catalan Atlas (1375):** Created by Abraham Cresques, the Catalan Atlas depicts Mansa Musa holding a gold nugget — the most famous European medieval representation of an African ruler. However, what African empires themselves mapped remains largely unstudied in Western cartographic history.

**Timbuktu manuscripts:** Hundreds of thousands of manuscripts from the medieval Mali and Songhai empires survive, covering astronomy, geography, law, and medicine. The Ahmed Baba Institute in Timbuktu houses these, many of which contain geographic and cartographic knowledge. During the 2012–2013 occupation of Timbuktu by Islamist militants, many manuscripts were destroyed or hidden — a cartographic erasure event.

**What was mapped:** West African empires maintained trade routes across the Sahara, river navigation knowledge on the Niger, and city planning. The urban layout of medieval Timbuktu and Djenne reflects sophisticated spatial organization. Oral griot traditions mapped genealogies to territories.

### 1.7 The 1507 Waldseemüller Map — Naming "America"

**Date:** April 1507

**Place:** Saint-Dié-des-Vosges, Lorraine (Holy Roman Empire). Map now held at the Library of Congress, Washington DC (**38.8887, -77.0046**)

**What it is:** The *Universalis Cosmographia* by Martin Waldseemüller — the first map to use the name "America," placed on what is now South America, named after Amerigo Vespucci. It was also the first map to depict the Americas as a landmass separate from Asia, showing a new (Pacific) ocean.

**The erasure:** The continent already had millions of inhabitants with their own names for the land. The Kuna people called it *Abya Yala*; the Haudenosaunee knew it as *Turtle Island*; the Anishinaabe had *Turtle Island* (*Mishiike Minisi*); the Inca knew their empire as *Tawantinsuyu*. The Waldseemüller map's naming was a unilateral act — "America" was imposed without consultation, and over 500 years later, the indigenous names for the continents remain largely absent from official cartography.

The map itself acknowledges this tension: its inscription describes the inhabitants as "going about in no otherwise than as their mothers bore them" (naked), and places them in a European cosmological framework — Virgil's prophecy, Ptolemy's geography. Waldseemüller's cartographic innovation was also a cartographic annexation.

The inscription on the map referencing Amerigo Vespucci shows explicit European bias: naming a continent after a European man who visited it briefly, rather than its inhabitants who had lived there for millennia.

## 2. Cartographic Erasure of Indigenous Sovereignty {#2}

### 2.1 Terra Nullius — The Legal Fiction of "Empty Land"

**Key dates:**
- 1452–1493: Papal bulls (*Dum Diversas*, *Romanus Pontifex*, *Inter caetera*) establish the legal framework
- 1765: William Blackstone articulates "desert and uncultivated" land doctrine
- 1758: Emer de Vattel draws distinction between cultivated land (legitimate claim) and nomadic land (open to colonization)
- 1788: British colonization of Australia
- 1819: First Australian legal use of terra nullius concept (Barron Field vs. Lachlan Macquarie tax dispute)
- 1889: *Cooper v Stuart* — Privy Council declares NSW "a tract of territory practically unoccupied, without settled inhabitants or settled law"
- 1975: International Court of Justice advisory opinion on Western Sahara — finds area was NOT terra nullius
- 1992: *Mabo v Queensland (No 2)* — Australia's High Court rejects "enlarged notion of terra nullius"

**How maps reinforced the doctrine:** Colonial maps showed inhabited territories as blank spaces — "unexplored" or "uninhabited." The blank space on the map was a visual argument for legal emptiness. Australian colonial maps did not show Aboriginal territorial boundaries, songline routes, or language group territories. The absence was the violence.

In British Columbia, Lieutenant Governor Joseph Trutch insisted First Nations had never owned land — consequently, most of BC remains unceded territory. The map of "Crown Land" literally erased pre-existing indigenous tenure systems.

**Places where terra nullius was explicitly applied:**
- Australia (1788) — overturned by Mabo (1992)
- British Columbia, Canada — much remains unceded
- Western Sahara — ICJ ruled in 1975 it was NOT terra nullius at time of Spanish colonization
- Eastern Greenland — Norway claimed it as terra nullius in 1931, Permanent Court rejected this

**Sources:**
- Wikipedia: "Terra nullius" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terra_nullius
- *Mabo v Queensland (No 2)* [1992] HCA 23
- *Tsilhqot'in Nation v British Columbia* [2014] SCC 44
- ICJ Advisory Opinion on Western Sahara (1975)

### 2.2 The Doctrine of Discovery

**Key dates:**
- 1452: Papal bull *Dum Diversas* — authorizes Portugal to "subjugate Saracens and pagans"
- 1455: *Romanus Pontifex* — extends Portugal's rights in West Africa
- 1493: *Inter caetera* — draws line dividing non-Christian world between Spain and Portugal
- 1494: Treaty of Tordesillas
- 1823: *Johnson v. McIntosh* — US Supreme Court Chief Justice John Marshall codifies the Doctrine of Discovery into US law
- 2023: Vatican formally repudiates the Doctrine of Discovery (March 30, 2023)

**The cartographic-legal operation:** The doctrine held that "discovery" of lands unknown to Europeans gave the discovering nation title against other European nations. Indigenous peoples retained a "right of occupancy" but lost sovereignty. This was a legal theory that operated through maps — the act of placing a territory on a European map was simultaneously an act of claiming it.

In *Johnson v. McIntosh* (1823), the Piankeshaw tribe had sold land — but the court ruled their sale invalid because only the discovering nation could transfer title. The Piankeshaw were not party to the case. "No Indian voices were heard in a case which had, and continues to have, profound effects on Indian property rights." (Allison Dussias)

In the 2005 case *City of Sherrill v. Oneida Indian Nation of New York*, Justice Ginsburg cited the doctrine — showing its continuing legal force into the 21st century.

On November 3, 2016, 524 clergy publicly burned copies of *Inter caetera* at the Standing Rock protests.

**Sources:**
- Wikipedia: "Discovery doctrine" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_doctrine
- *Johnson v. McIntosh*, 21 U.S. 543 (1823)
- Miller, Ruru, Behrendt, Lindberg, *Discovering Indigenous Lands: The Doctrine of Discovery in the English Colonies* (2010), Oxford University Press
- Vatican statement (March 30, 2023) — https://press.vatican.va/content/salastampa/en/bollettino/pubblico/2023/03/30/230330b.html

### 2.4 The Indian Removal Act and the Trail of Tears

**Date:** Indian Removal Act signed May 28, 1830; Trail of Tears forced relocation 1830–1850

**Places:**
- Cherokee Nation, Georgia (**34.4, -84.1**) — subject of *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* (1831)
- Trail of Tears route: Southeastern US → Oklahoma (**35.5, -98.0**)
- New Echota, Georgia (**34.54, -84.91**) — where the fraudulent Treaty of New Echota was signed (1835)

**The cartographic operation:** The Indian Removal Act was fundamentally a mapping exercise. It required:
1. Surveying and mapping "Indian Territory" (present-day Oklahoma) — drawing lines on maps to create containment zones
2. Mapping the "removal routes" — the Trail of Tears was a cartographically planned mass deportation
3. Erasing Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Creek, and Seminole territories from maps of the Southeast
4. The 1823 *Johnson v. McIntosh* decision (doctrine of discovery) provided the legal-cartographic justification

**Key case law:** In *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* (1831), the Supreme Court declared the Cherokee a "domestic dependent nation" with no standing to sue Georgia. In *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832), Marshall ruled that Georgia had no authority within Cherokee territory — but President Andrew Jackson famously defied the ruling. The maps had already been drawn; the legal arguments were irrelevant to the cartographic reality on the ground.

An estimated 4,000 Cherokee died on the Trail of Tears. This was a map-enabled genocide.

### 2.5 Residential School Mapping

**Dates:** Canadian Indian Residential School System operated from the 1880s through 1996 (last school closed)

**Places (selected):**
- Kamloops Indian Residential School, BC (**50.68, -120.34**) — 215 unmarked graves discovered in 2021
- Marieval Indian Residential School, SK (**50.58, -102.65**) — 751 unmarked graves discovered in 2021
- St. Mary's Mission, BC (**49.0, -122.2**)
- Shubenacadie Residential School, NS (**45.12, -63.35**)
- Gordon's Residential School, SK (**50.55, -104.8**)
- Mohawk Institute Residential School, ON (**43.12, -80.24**)

**The cartographic absence:** Official government maps of Canada systematically excluded residential schools. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada (TRC) documented over 139 residential schools, but finding their locations requires specialized historical research — they were not on standard maps. The schools themselves were often located near indigenous communities but were deliberately positioned to remove children from their families.

The TRC's 2015 report explicitly called for mapping the residential school system and the unmarked graves. The discovery at Kamloops in May 2021, using ground-penetrating radar, confirmed what survivors had been saying for decades — that children who died at the schools were buried in unmarked graves that did not appear on any map.

**Why this is a cartographic issue:** The absence from maps was not accidental. It was part of the paper genocide — the systematic erasure of indigenous presence from official records. Mapping the schools and the graves is now a central part of truth and reconciliation.

### 2.6 The Tsilhqot'in Decision (2014) — First Declaration of Aboriginal Title in Canadian History

**Date:** June 26, 2014

**Place:** Xeni Gwet'in traditional territory, central British Columbia
- Nemiah Valley (**51.4833, -124.2167**)
- Area of title: 1,750 km²
- Xeni Gwet'in First Nation (**51.5, -124.2**)

**The conflict:** In 1983, British Columbia issued a logging license to Carrier Lumber. The Xeni Gwet'in band of the Tsilhqot'in Nation blockaded the area. After 30+ years of litigation, the Supreme Court of Canada unanimously declared Aboriginal title — the first such declaration in Canadian history.

**The role of Indigenous mapping:** The Tsilhqot'in case relied on extensive indigenous mapping of traditional territory — oral histories, land use patterns, hunting and trapping routes, village sites, spiritual locations. The Court explicitly affirmed that Aboriginal title contains the right to "decide how the land will be used" and to "proactively use and manage the land, including its natural resources." This is a judicial recognition that indigenous maps constitute valid legal evidence of sovereignty.

**Key people:** Chief Justice Beverley McLachlin wrote the unanimous decision. Roger William (Xeni Gwet'in Chief) led the legal challenge.

**Significance:** The Tsilhqot'in decision explicitly stated that "the doctrine of *terra nullius* never applied in Canada." This overturned centuries of cartographic-legal fiction.

**Sources:**
- *Tsilhqot'in Nation v British Columbia*, 2014 SCC 44

## 3. Decolonial Counter-Mapping Movements {#3}

### 3.1 Māori Land Court Maps and the Waitangi Tribunal

**Dates:** Native Land Court established 1865 (renamed Māori Land Court, 1954). Waitangi Tribunal established 1975.

**Places:**
- Waitangi, Bay of Islands (**-35.2667, 174.0833**) — Treaty of Waitangi signing site (1840)
- Wellington (**-41.2865, 174.7762**) — Waitangi Tribunal and Māori Land Court offices

**Native Land Court as cartographic dispossession:** Established in 1865, the Native Land Court's explicit purpose was "to encourage the extinction of such [native] proprietary customs." It converted collectively-owned Māori customary land into individual titles recognizable under English law, enabling Pākehā (European) purchase. By limiting titles to ten owners maximum, it shattered communal landholding systems.

By 1939, Māori retained just 1% of the South Island and 9% of the North Island. The court's minute books (1862–1900) are now inscribed on the UNESCO Memory of the World register — a recognition of their dual role as instruments of dispossession and as the most detailed surviving records of Māori land claims.

**Waitangi Tribunal as counter-mapping:** Since 1975, the Waitangi Tribunal has heard claims of Crown breaches of the Treaty of Waitangi. Traditional Māori maps, oral histories, and *whakapapa* (genealogies tied to land) have been crucial evidence. The Tribunal has recommended land returns, financial compensation, and co-governance arrangements. In 1993, *Te Ture Whenua Māori Act* expanded the Māori Land Court's jurisdiction to protect Māori land from alienation.

**Key legal developments:**
- 1987: *New Zealand Maori Council v Attorney-General* (SOE case) — Treaty principles become part of NZ law
- 1993: *Te Ture Whenua Māori Act* — recognizes Māori land as *taonga tuku iho* (treasure to be handed down)
- Māori Land Court minute books on UNESCO Memory of the World register (2012)

**Sources:**
- Wikipedia: "Māori Land Court" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_Land_Court
- David Williams, *Te Kooti Tango Whenua: The Native Land Court 1864-1909* (1999)
- Waitangi Tribunal — https://www.waitangitribunal.govt.nz

### 3.2 Zapatista Autonomous Municipalities

**Dates:** January 1, 1994 — present

**Places:**
- San Cristóbal de las Casas, Chiapas (**16.7370, -92.6376**) — EZLN headquarters region
- Lacandon Jungle region (**16.5, -91.5**)
- Caracoles (regional centers): Oventic (**16.8667, -92.5500**), La Realidad (**16.5000, -91.5000**), Morelia (**16.7167, -92.6167**)

**What happened:** On January 1, 1994 (the day NAFTA took effect), the EZLN seized six towns in Chiapas, demanding indigenous rights, land reform, and democratization. After the ceasefire, they unilaterally declared 32 "Rebel Zapatista Autonomous Municipalities" (*MAREZ*) and five regional centers (*Caracoles*).

**The mapping dimension:** The Zapatistas have mapped territory *outside* the Mexican state's administrative grid. Their autonomous municipalities don't correspond to official municipal boundaries — they follow indigenous community organization, watersheds, and traditional territories. This is a lived counter-cartography: the Zapatista map of Chiapas looks different from the government's map. The Zapatistas have also used digital media extensively — their communiqués, the "Other Campaign" (2006), and international solidarity have created a virtual geography of support that extends well beyond Chiapas.

**Key people:** Subcomandante Insurgente Galeano (formerly Subcomandante Marcos), Comandanta Ramona, Subcomandante Moisés

**Why it matters:** The Zapatistas demonstrate that mapping can be a weapon of the weak — by creating autonomous zones with their own spatial logic, they challenge the state's monopoly on defining territory. Their system is decentralized, community-governed, and resists incorporation into official mapping systems.

**Sources:**
- Wikipedia: "Zapatista Army of National Liberation"
- Sixth Declaration of the Lacandon Jungle (2005)
- Enlace Zapatista — http://enlacezapatista.ezln.org.mx

### 3.3 Sámi Parliaments and Traditional Reindeer Herding Routes

**Dates:** Sámi Parliaments established: Norway (1989), Sweden (1993), Finland (1973/1996)

**Places:** Sápmi (traditional Sámi territory spanning Norway, Sweden, Finland, and Russia's Kola Peninsula):
- Karasjok, Norway (**69.47, 25.51**) — Sámi Parliament of Norway
- Inari, Finland (**68.90, 27.02**) — Sámi Parliament of Finland
- Kiruna, Sweden (**67.85, 20.22**) — Sámi Parliament of Sweden

**The mapping task:** Sámi reindeer herding follows ancient migratory routes that cross national borders. Norway, Sweden, Finland, and Russia have drawn borders through Sápmi since the 1751 Strömstad Treaty. These borders cut through traditional herding routes, seasonal grazing lands, and sacred sites. Sámi parliaments have developed detailed GIS maps of traditional territories to assert land rights and protect herding routes from mining, logging, wind farms, and infrastructure projects.

**Conflict sites:** The Gállok iron mine in Sweden (**66.7, 18.5**) and the Fosen wind farm in Norway (**63.6, 9.5**) are contested sites where Sámi mapping has been central to legal challenges.

### 3.5 Palestinian Mapping of Nakba-Vanished Villages

**Dates:** 1948 (Nakba) — ongoing

**Places (selected destroyed villages):**
- Lifta, West Jerusalem (**31.7958, 35.1967**) — one of the few not completely rebuilt over, now partially standing ruins
- Deir Yassin (**31.7867, 35.1767**) — site of the April 1948 massacre
- Tantura (**32.6100, 34.9000**) — coastal village, site of massacre documented by Teddy Katz
- al-Lydda/Lod (**31.9566, 34.8966**) — site of mass expulsion July 1948
- Saffuriyya (**32.7500, 35.2833**) — depopulated July 1948
- Approximately 500+ villages were depopulated and physically destroyed

**The mapping work:** Palestinian researchers — notably Walid Khalidi (*All That Remains*, 1992) and Salman Abu Sitta (*Atlas of Palestine*) — have meticulously reconstructed the locations, populations, land ownership, and economic activities of the destroyed villages. Zochrot (an Israeli NGO) has created an interactive map at https://zochrot.org marking each destroyed village's location.

**Key data points:**
- 750,000+ Palestinians expelled or fled (1947–1949)
- 78% of Mandatory Palestine came under Israeli control by 1949
- 7.2 million Palestinian refugees and descendants currently registered with UNRWA
- The "key" and the "deed" are central Palestinian symbols — representing the maps, land deeds, and house keys that refugees kept

**Why this is counter-mapping:** The Israeli state's Nakba Law (2011) allows the finance minister to reduce state funding to institutions that mark Nakba Day. The mapping of vanished villages is therefore a direct act of political resistance — asserting cartographic memory against state erasure. Many destroyed villages do not appear on official Israeli maps; Palestinian maps restore them to cartographic visibility.

### 3.6 Hawaiian Sovereignty Movement and Ahupua'a Mapping

**Dates:** Hawaiian Kingdom overthrown 1893; annexed by US 1898; sovereignty movement ongoing

**Places:**
- Honolulu, Oʻahu (**21.3069, -157.8583**)
- Mauna Kea, Hawaiʻi Island (**19.8207, -155.4681**) — site of 2019 protests against Thirty Meter Telescope
- Iolani Palace (**21.3068, -157.8589**) — only royal palace on US soil

**Ahupuaʻa system:** Traditional Hawaiian land divisions (*ahupuaʻa*) typically followed watershed boundaries from mountain (*mauka*) to sea (*makai*), creating self-sufficient ecological units. The Great Mahele of 1848 dismantled this system, introducing Western private property concepts. Today, Hawaiian sovereignty activists use GIS to reconstruct ahupuaʻa boundaries and claim rights based on traditional land tenure.

**Key figures:** The Office of Hawaiian Affairs (OHA) has developed GIS databases of traditional ahupuaʻa. The Kīpuka Database created by OHA maps Native Hawaiian land parcels and land claim histories. Community groups like the Protect Kahoʻolawe ʻOhana have mapped sacred and archaeological sites.

**Mauna Kea protests (2019):** The conflict over the Thirty Meter Telescope on Mauna Kea brought mapping to international attention. Kānaka Maoli (Native Hawaiian) protesters asserted that Mauna Kea, as a sacred site within the traditional land system, could not be unilaterally developed.

## 4. Contemporary Indigenous GIS and Digital Sovereignty {#4}

### 4.1 Aboriginal Land Rights Act (NT) 1976 and Indigenous Mapping in Australia

**Date:** Aboriginal Land Rights (Northern Territory) Act 1976

**Places:** Northern Territory, Australia — specifically:
- Uluru-Kata Tjuta National Park (**-25.3444, 131.0369**) — handed back to Anangu traditional owners in 1985
- Kakadu National Park (**-12.5, 132.5**) — joint management with Aboriginal traditional owners
- Arnhem Land (**-12.5, 134.5**) — Aboriginal freehold land

**The mapping dimension:** The Land Rights Act required Aboriginal people to demonstrate "traditional ownership" through evidence of spiritual affiliation and traditional land use. This created an unprecedented demand for indigenous mapping — recording songlines, sacred sites, hunting grounds, and clan boundaries in forms admissible to the Aboriginal Land Commissioner. This process was simultaneously empowering (restoring land) and problematic (forcing indigenous knowledge into colonial legal frameworks).

**Key outcomes:** Approximately 50% of the Northern Territory is now Aboriginal freehold land. The mapping work done for land claims created archives of indigenous geographic knowledge that, while created for legal purposes, now serve as cultural preservation tools.

**Sources:**
- *Aboriginal Land Rights (Northern Territory) Act 1976* (Cth)
- Wikipedia: "Indigenous land rights in Australia"

### 4.2 Nunavut — The Largest Indigenous Land Claim Settlement in History

**Date:** April 1, 1999 — Nunavut officially separated from the Northwest Territories

**Place:** Nunavut Territory, Canada:
- Iqaluit (**63.7467, -68.5170**) — capital
- Area: 2,093,190 km² (larger than Mexico, fifth-largest subnational entity in the world)
- Population: 36,858 (2021), approximately 85% Inuit

**The mapping achievement:** The 1993 Nunavut Land Claims Agreement and the 1999 Nunavut Act created a territory governed by Inuit through a public government. The boundaries were drawn in 1993 after decades of negotiation informed by Inuit mapping of traditional territories, hunting grounds, travel routes, and community locations. A 1982 plebiscite had supported division of the Northwest Territories.

Nunavut is the first major change to Canada's political map since Newfoundland joined Confederation in 1949. It represents a rare example of decolonization through cartography — the map was redrawn to reflect indigenous territorial reality rather than colonial administrative convenience.

**Why it matters:** Nunavut proves that maps can be instruments of restitution, not just dispossession. The territory's creation required decades of community-based mapping, negotiation, and legal work. The result is a territory the size of Mexico where Inuit are the majority and Inuktitut is an official language.

**Key people:** Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami (ITK), John Amagoalik ("father of Nunavut")

**Sources:**
- Wikipedia: "Nunavut" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nunavut
- *Nunavut Land Claims Agreement Act* (1993)
- *Nunavut Act* (1993)

### 4.3 Ogiek People of Kenya — African Court Ruling (2017)

**Date:** May 26, 2017 (landmark ruling); June 23, 2022 (reparations ruling)

**Place:** Mau Forest, Kenya (**-0.5, 35.5**), Mount Elgon (**1.13, 34.55**)

**Who they are:** The Ogiek (Okiek) are one of the oldest indigenous communities in East Africa, hunter-gatherers and honey-harvesters inhabiting the highland forests of Kenya for an estimated 4,000+ years. They predate most modern ethnolinguistic groups in the region.

**The eviction:** The Kenyan government repeatedly evicted Ogiek from the Mau Forest, claiming they were destroying the water catchment (despite the Ogiek's documented sustainable forest management practices). The government issued land titles to non-Ogiek settlers and logging companies (including Timsales Ltd, partly owned by relatives of former presidents Kenyatta and Moi).

**The mapping and the ruling:** Ogiek community organizations, supported by Minority Rights Group International, Survival International, and the Ogiek Peoples' Development Program, used participatory GIS mapping to document ancestral land use, sacred sites, beekeeping locations, and hunting grounds. The African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights in Arusha, Tanzania, ruled that Kenya had violated the Ogiek's rights to land, religion, culture, and development. In the June 2022 reparations ruling, the court ordered Kenya to pay 157,850,000 Kenyan shillings and recognize Ogiek indigeneity.

**Landmark significance:** This was the first indigenous land rights case decided by the African Court. Community mapping was central to the evidence.

**Sources:**
- Wikipedia: "Okiek people" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okiek_people
- Human Rights Watch: "Kenya: Landmark Ruling Restores Ogiek Land Rights" (May 26, 2017) — https://www.hrw.org/news/2017/05/26/kenya-landmark-ruling-restores-ogiek-land-rights
- Grist: "Indigenous Ogiek win 'landmark' reparations ruling from African Court" (June 2022)
- African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights, Application No. 006/2012

### 4.4 Sarayaku People of Ecuador — The "Living Forest" (Kawsak Sacha)

**Dates:** 2012 (case filed with Inter-American Court); 2018 (declaration of Kawsak Sacha)

**Place:** Sarayaku territory, Pastaza Province, Ecuador (**-1.7333, -77.4833**)

**What happened:** The Sarayaku Kichwa people won a landmark case at the Inter-American Court of Human Rights in 2012 when the court ruled that Ecuador had violated their rights by allowing an Argentine oil company (CGC) to conduct seismic testing on their territory without consent. The court ordered the removal of 1,433 kg of pentolite explosives buried by the company.

**The "Living Forest" (*Kawsak Sacha*):** In 2018, the Sarayaku presented a formal declaration of *Kawsak Sacha* (Living Forest) — a legal and philosophical framework that recognizes the forest as a living being with rights, inhabited by spirit beings (*Supay*, *Amazanga*). Their 3D community mapping project created detailed territorial maps showing the boundaries of the Living Forest, sacred sites, hunting grounds, and spiritual territories. The maps combine GPS/GIS with traditional knowledge, creating a visual-legal argument for territorial autonomy.

**Why it matters:** The Sarayaku 3D maps are not just spatial representations — they are sovereignty documents. By mapping their territory in three dimensions with GIS precision while incorporating traditional ecological knowledge, they have created a model for indigenous digital sovereignty.

**Coordinates of note:** The Sarayaku territory borders are at approximately **-1.5 to -2.0 latitude, -77.0 to -78.0 longitude** in the Ecuadorian Amazon, along the Bobonaza River.

**Sources:**
- *Kichwa Indigenous People of Sarayaku v. Ecuador*, Inter-American Court of Human Rights (2012)
- Kawsak Sacha Declaration (2018)
- Amazon Watch: Sarayaku — https://amazonwatch.org/work/Sarayaku

## 5. Environmental Decolonization Through Mapping {#5}

### 5.1 Dakota Access Pipeline Protests (Standing Rock, 2016)

**Dates:** April 2016 – February 2017

**Place:**
- Standing Rock Sioux Reservation (**45.75, -101.2**)
- Lake Oahe crossing (**46.6, -100.7**)
- Cannon Ball, North Dakota (**46.39, -100.59**) — Sacred Stone Camp location
- Pipeline route: Bakken oil fields (North Dakota) to Patoka, Illinois — 1,886 km (1,172 miles)

**The contested mapping:** Energy Transfer Partners (ETP) originally planned the pipeline to cross the Missouri River north of Bismarck, but the US Army Corps of Engineers rejected this route due to risk to Bismarck's water supply. The route was moved to cross the river just half a mile upstream of the Standing Rock Sioux Reservation, threatening the tribe's primary water source and sacred sites. This rerouting was a cartographic decision that explicitly transferred environmental risk from a predominantly white community to an indigenous one.

**The resistance:** The Standing Rock Sioux Tribe's legal challenge centered on the lack of proper environmental and cultural impact studies. Tribal maps identified:
- Sacred stone features and burial grounds in the pipeline path
- The pipeline's proposed crossing under Lake Oahe (a reservoir on the Missouri River)
- Treaty boundaries from the 1851 Treaty of Traverse des Sioux and 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie

On September 20, 2016, Chairman David Archambault II addressed the UN Human Rights Council in Geneva, citing the Treaties and the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples.

**What happened:** In December 2016, the Army Corps denied the easement for the Lake Oahe crossing. In January 2017, President Trump issued a memorandum advancing the pipeline. The pipeline was completed and began operating in June 2017. However, in March 2020, a federal court ordered a full Environmental Impact Statement, and in July 2020, the pipeline was ordered to shut down pending the review.

**Key data:** 800+ arrests, 300+ injuries, $38 million+ in policing costs. 524 clergy burned copies of the papal bull *Inter caetera* at the protest site on November 3, 2016 — explicitly linking the protest to the Doctrine of Discovery.

**Sources:**
- Wikipedia: "Dakota Access Pipeline protests"
- Earthjustice: Standing Rock Sioux Tribe v. US Army Corps of Engineers
- UN Human Rights Council presentation (September 20, 2016)
- Standing Rock Sioux Tribe resolution and legal filings

### 5.2 Yasuní-ITT Initiative, Ecuador

**Dates:** 2007–2013

**Place:** Yasuní National Park, Ecuador (**-1.0, -76.0**)
- The Ishpingo-Tambococha-Tiputini (ITT) oil block lies beneath one of the most biodiverse places on Earth
- Home to Tagaeri and Taromenane — two indigenous groups living in voluntary isolation

**The initiative:** President Rafael Correa proposed leaving 846 million barrels of oil in the ground in exchange for $3.6 billion in international contributions (half the oil's market value). The Yasuní-ITT Trust Fund was established under UNDP administration. This was a fundamentally cartographic act — drawing a line on the map and saying "no drilling here" — justified by both environmental science and indigenous territorial rights.

**What happened:** In 2013, Correa cancelled the initiative, citing insufficient international contributions. The Ecuadorian government subsequently approved drilling. The Tagaeri and Taromenane remain in voluntary isolation, their territories mapped by outsiders but recognized by international law.

**Key coordinates:** ITT Block is approximately **-0.5 to -1.5 latitude, -75.5 to -76.5 longitude**

### 5.3 Brazilian Amazon — Indigenous Territories as Deforestation Buffers

**Places:**
- Xingu Indigenous Park (**-12.0, -53.5**) — 26,000 km², home to 16 ethnic groups
- Yanomami Territory (**2.0, -63.5**) — 96,650 km², largest indigenous territory in Brazil
- Kayapó Territory (**-7.5, -51.5**)

**The data:** Scientific studies consistently show that indigenous territories have lower deforestation rates than surrounding areas. Indigenous territories in the Brazilian Amazon cover approximately 13% of the country's land area but contain over 25% of the above-ground carbon. The mapping and demarcation of indigenous territories (a constitutional right under Brazil's 1988 Constitution) is one of the most effective environmental protection strategies available.

The Brazilian National Indian Foundation (FUNAI) and the Ministry of Justice have demarcated over 700 indigenous territories. Under President Jair Bolsonaro (2019–2022), demarcations were halted and deforestation surged.

**Key organizations:** Instituto Socioambiental (ISA) maintains detailed GIS databases of indigenous territories. RAISG (Amazonian Network of Georeferenced Socio-Environmental Information) maps indigenous lands across nine Amazonian countries.

**Sources:**
- Instituto Socioambiental (ISA) — https://www.socioambiental.org
- RAISG — https://www.amazoniasocioambiental.org
- RAISG, *Amazonia Under Pressure* (2020 report)

### 5.4 Murray River First Nations — Mapping for Water Rights, Australia

**Places:** Murray-Darling Basin, southeastern Australia:
- Murray River (**-35.5, 144.9** to **-34.2, 142.2**)
- Coorong, South Australia (**-35.6, 138.9**) — Ramsar wetland, Ngarrindjeri traditional territory

**The mapping work:** First Nations along the Murray River (including the Ngarrindjeri, Yorta Yorta, and others) have used community mapping to assert water rights under the Murray-Darling Basin Plan. The 2007 *Native Title Act* amendments and the *Water Act* have created some avenues for indigenous water allocations, but indigenous water rights remain severely underrepresented in Australia's water market.

**Key issues:**
- Aboriginal people hold less than 1% of Australian water entitlements despite making up over 3% of the population
- Traditional ecological knowledge of water flows, fish migration, and flood patterns is being mapped alongside Western hydrological data
- The Ngarrindjeri *Kungun Ngarrindjeri Yunnan* (Listen to Ngarrindjeri People Talking) Agreement (2009) with the SA government is a model for indigenous water co-management

## 6. Connections to piggPin's Philosophy {#6}

### 6.1 P2P (Peer-to-Peer)

The pre-colonial mapping traditions documented above are inherently peer-to-peer:
- Songlines are shared knowledge between adjacent language groups — each group knows its section and passes travelers to the next group
- Marshall Islands stick charts were shared among navigator families, with knowledge passed from master to apprentice
- Polynesian navigation was taught through apprenticeship, not institutions
- Winter counts were kept by designated community keepers, passed to successors

piggin's P2P encrypted mapping model echoes these traditions: decentralized, community-controlled, resistant to centralized authority.

### 6.2 Encryption

Many indigenous mapping systems were inherently encrypted:
- Songlines are sung in language — incomprehensible to those outside the culture
- Quipus could only be read by their keeper — a physical encryption system
- Stick charts were individualized — "the individual navigator who made the chart was the only person who could fully interpret and use it"
- Winter counts served as mnemonic prompts for oral narratives held by the keeper

Encryption protects indigenous knowledge from extraction and commodification. piggin's end-to-end encryption provides the same protection in digital form.

### 6.3 Community-Owned

Every example in this report features community-controlled knowledge:
- Quipus remain in active communal use in Tupicocha and Rapaz, Peru — handled only by pairs of dignitaries
- The Zapatista autonomous municipalities explicitly reject state incorporation
- Tribal GIS programs enforce data sovereignty
- The Sarayaku "Living Forest" declaration asserts community ownership of territorial knowledge

### 6.4 Palimpsest

A palimpsest is a manuscript page from which text has been scraped to make room for new text, but where traces of the original remain. This is the perfect metaphor for decolonial mapping:
- Palestinian villages scraped from the landscape but mapped by survivors — the old names visible beneath the new Hebrew toponyms
- Songlines overwritten by highways but still sung — the old route survives in the song even when the surface has been asphalted
- *Terra nullius* declared over inhabited land — but the Aboriginal Land Rights Act and Native Title now restore recognition of what was always there
- The Waldseemüller map literally overwrote indigenous names for the continent — but Abya Yala and Turtle Island persist

piggPin as a "palimpsest" mapping tool would allow multiple layers of place-naming, multiple territorial claims, and the visibility of erased histories alongside current administrative realities.

### 6.5 Privacy and Sovereignty

The recurring theme across all these examples is that **the right to map is the right to exist** — and conversely, **to be erased from the map is to be erased from legal and political existence**. piggPin's encrypted P2P architecture provides indigenous communities with a tool that:
- Cannot be surveilled by extractive industries or hostile states
- Cannot be altered or erased by centralized authorities
- Can exist outside colonial legal frameworks
- Allows communities to control who sees their territorial knowledge
